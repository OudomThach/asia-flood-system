2.1 HYDROLOGICAL INGESTION & DATA SOURCES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT I DO:
  - Built an automated, asynchronous ingestion engine in `data_intake.py`.
  - Ingests real-time river discharge (m3/s) from the Copernicus GloFAS 
    (Global Flood Awareness System) dataset via Open-Meteo Flood API.
  - Ingests short-term and forecasted precipitation (mm) via Open-Meteo Weather API.
  - Validates and parses telemetry into type-safe Pydantic models with physical 
    boundary assertions (discharge >= 0, precipitation >= 0).

WHY I DO THIS (The Engineering Rationale):
  - Why GloFAS + Open-Meteo?
    Local physical river gauge data in rural Cambodia and across transboundary Asian 
    river systems is often siloed, manually recorded, or delayed. Copernicus GloFAS 
    provides authoritative, calibrated, satellite-and-hydrological modeling across 
    the entire Asian landmass. Open-Meteo exposes this data via high-speed, rate-friendly REST endpoints.
  - Why combine river discharge AND rainfall?
    Flash floods across Asia occur through two distinct mechanisms:
    1. Upstream river surges (large volume moving slowly downriver from high basins).
    2. Local intense tropical monsoon downpours / typhoons (flash flooding in urban/rural basins).
    Monitoring discharge alone misses flash rainstorms; monitoring rain alone 
    misses upstream river surges. Combining both provides holistic situational awareness.

2.3 HYDROLOGICAL PHYSICS: DISCHARGE (m3/s) VS WATER LEVEL (m)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT I DO:
  - Chose volumetric river discharge (cubic meters per second, m3/s) as the primary 
    hydrological metric rather than raw water gauge height (meters).

WHY I DO THIS (The Scientific Rationale):
  - Why not raw meters (m)?
    Water level (m) is strictly local and depends entirely on the riverbed geometry 
    at that exact cross-section (a narrow gorge has a huge water level rise with 
    small volume, whereas a wide floodplain has a small water level rise with massive volume).
  - Volumetric Discharge (m3/s) is conservation of mass: The volume of water entering 
    upstream must pass downstream. It enables direct physical comparison across stations.
  - Respecting MRC Standards: While Mekong River Commission (MRC) sets Kratie warning 
    levels at 22.00m and alarm at 23.00m, converting meters to m3/s without a calibrated 
    bathymetric rating curve introduces mathematical error. Using GloFAS discharge directly 
    is physically grounded and standardized across the basin.

    * Brahmaputra (India/BD): Baseline 15,000 m³/s | Caution 22,000 m³/s | Danger 30,000 m³/s
    * Indus Basin (Pakistan): Baseline 9,000 m³/s | Caution 14,000 m³/s | Danger 20,000 m³/s

  - The Risk Classification Engine `evaluate(reading, area=None)` dynamically resolves the station's

  - No "One Size Fits All" in Hydrology:
    A discharge of 22,000 m³/s is a critical disaster flood for the Mekong or Indus, but is normal
    low-season flow for the mighty Yangtze (average ~30,000 m³/s) and an impossible ocean volume for
    a steep urban catchment like Jakarta's Ciliwung River (where 450 m³/s overflows city levees).
  - Profile-aware evaluation ensures zero false alarms for mega-rivers and life-saving sensitivity
    for small volcanic and urban watersheds.

2.5 FAULT RESILIENCE & ZERO FALSE ESCALATION (NFR3 / PRINCIPLE 1)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT I DO:
  - Built graceful fallback and staleness tagging in `data_intake.py` and `storage.py`.
  - If Open-Meteo or Copernicus APIs timeout or return HTTP 500/503:
    1. The system catches the exception and logs a structured warning.
    2. It retrieves the last valid cached reading from SQLite / in-memory cache.
    3. It sets `is_stale: true` on the telemetry model.
    4. The Risk Engine evaluates the stale data but NEVER artificially promotes it to Danger.

WHY I DO THIS (The Reliability Rationale):
  - Preventing "Cry Wolf" Syndrome: In disaster early warning systems, false alarms 
    destroy public trust. If people receive a false Danger alarm because a server timed out, 
    they will ignore real alerts during an actual flood.
  - Continuous Availability: The admin console and resident APIs continue serving the last 
    known state with full transparency regarding data freshness.

2.6 STORAGE, TIMEZONES & HIGH PERFORMANCE (SQLITE WAL + HOT CACHE)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WHAT I DO:
  - Engineered `storage.py` with multi-tier optimizations:
    1. Default database `asia_flood.db` (configurable via `FLOOD_DB_PATH` env override).
    2. SQLite in WAL Mode (Write-Ahead Logging).
    3. In-Memory Hot Cache (`_latest_reading_cache`, `_latest_risk_cache`, `_areas_cache`).
    4. Smart Write Deduplication (Anti-Spam Delta Gate).
    5. Additive migrations for `language`, `utc_offset_hours`, `timezone_name` in `areas`.
    6. Region-aware local time logging (`timestamp_local`, `dispatched_at_local`).
    7. Station seed logic with plausible per-basin baseline discharges across all 63 stations.

WHY I DO THIS (The Performance & Architecture Rationale):
  - Why SQLite WAL Mode instead of Postgres or MySQL?
    * Zero Configuration & Embedded Reliability: Eliminates external database network 
      failure points, credentials, and container dependencies.
    * WAL Mode allows concurrent non-blocking readers while background ingestion writes.
    * For 63 stations polled every 15 minutes, SQLite handles queries in <1ms.
  - Why Region-Aware Timestamps instead of hardcoded ICT?
    * Spanning 12 countries from Pakistan (PKT UTC+5) to China/Philippines/Malaysia (CST/PHT/MYT UTC+8)
      and Japan (JST UTC+9) requires local operational time context for field responders.

4. PROFESSOR "GRILL ME" DEFENSE SCRIPT (TOUGH QUESTIONS & WINNING ANSWERS)

Q1: "Oudom, what is the core purpose of your subsystem?"
A1: "I own the foundation of the platform: ingesting real-time hydrometeorological 
    data across 63 Asian stations in 12 countries, validating and persisting it in an optimized 
    SQLite WAL database, evaluating flood risk via a calibrated deterministic engine, 
    monitoring 14 compound multi-hazard drivers, and serving the administrator command dashboard."

Q2: "Why did you choose a rule-based engine over machine learning for risk classification?"
A2: "In life-critical emergency management, explainability and zero false alarms are paramount. 
    A rule-based engine produces 100% deterministic, auditable decisions with human-readable 
    explanations ('Discharge >= 22,000 m3/s') tied to a versioned rule (v1.0). An ML model 
    acts as a black box and risks hallucinations or false alarms that erode public trust."

Q3: "Why expand to 63 stations across 12 countries in Asia?"
A3: "Because Asia's hydrological, tectonic, and meteorological systems are intrinsically linked. 
    The rivers of South, East, and Southeast Asia (Indus, Ganges, Brahmaputra, Yangtze, Yellow, 
    Mekong, Red River, Chao Phraya, Irrawaddy) originate in High Mountain Asia / Tibetan Plateau. 
    Monsoons, typhoons, and glacial lake bursts cross international borders freely. Extending coverage 
    to 12 nations provides early lead time and complete regional situational awareness."

Q4: "What happens if Open-Meteo or GloFAS APIs crash during a storm?"
A4: "Per our Engineering Constitution Principle 1 (NFR3), the ingestion service gracefully catches 
    the failure, retrieves the last valid reading from our in-memory cache/SQLite, flags it as 
    `is_stale: true`, and logs a warning. The risk engine processes it without crashing, and 
    crucially, never escalates stale data to a false Danger alarm."

Q5: "Why did you choose SQLite with WAL mode instead of PostgreSQL?"
A5: "For our operational scale of 63 stations polled every 15 minutes, SQLite in WAL mode provides 
    zero-setup embedded reliability with no external network failure points. Paired with our 
    in-memory hot cache, it delivers sub-millisecond query responses while supporting non-blocking 
    concurrent reads and writes."

Q6: "What is Smart Write Deduplication in your storage layer?"
A6: "During dry seasons or stable conditions, sensors often report identical values consecutively. 
    Our deduplication logic updates the timestamp in memory but drops redundant disk writes, 
    preventing database bloat and optimizing disk I/O."

Q7: "How does your manual test alert feature work, and why is it isolated?"
A7: "Under FR10, administrators can trigger test broadcasts from the console to verify Telegram 
    and SMS subscriber channels. To prevent public panic, test alerts are tagged with `is_test: true` 
    and prefixed with `[TEST ALERT]`. They run through the dispatch pipeline and log to a dedicated 
    audit table, but NEVER modify the real hydrological risk level of any station."

Q8: "Why did you expand scope to earthquakes, typhoons, glacial lake outbursts (GLOF), and landslides?"
A8: "Because catastrophic Mekong floods in Southeast Asia are compound cascading hazards. A magnitude 6.5+ 
    earthquake in Myanmar, Yunnan, or Laos can structurally compromise or breach Mekong cascade dams; an active 
    super typhoon dumps hundreds of millimeters of rain onto steep catchments; high-altitude glacial lakes in the 
    Tibetan Plateau (Lancang headwaters) can breach their moraines (GLOF); and mountain landslides can dam river 
    canyons, creating sudden outburst flash-floods. Integrating real-time USGS, NOAA/JTWC, and NASA LHASA APIs 
    enables the system to calculate compound cascading threat multipliers rather than treating floods in isolation."

Q9: "What physical mathematical formulas do you use for dam breach and storm surges?"
A9: "We use the Froehlich (2008) empirical dam break equation:
    Q_p = 0.607 * (V_w ** 0.295) * (h_w ** 1.24)
    where V_w is reservoir storage volume (m³) and h_w is hydraulic head above breach (m), coupled with downstream 
    attenuation routing. For typhoon catchment influx, we apply the rational surge equation:
    ΔQ = (C * I * A) / 3.6
    where C is the runoff coefficient, I is rainfall intensity (mm/h), and A is catchment area (km²)."

Q10: "Can you explain all 14 direct and indirect flash-flood drivers modeled in your system? What causes sudden floods?"
A10: "Our platform models every physical, geological, meteorological, cryospheric, volcanic, coastal marine, 
     civil infrastructure, and urban micro-catchment driver that can directly or indirectly trigger or amplify a flash flood:
     1. Seismic Shocks & Dam Breach: USGS Mw >= 4.0 near Mekong cascade dams triggering Froehlich peak breach discharge.
     2. Tropical Cyclones & Super Typhoons: JTWC/NOAA gale-force rain swaths dumping hundreds of mm into 3S catchments.
     3. Glacial Lake Outburst Floods (GLOF): Moraine-dammed cryospheric lake collapses in High Mountain Asia / Tibetan Plateau.
     4. Landslide Debris Canyon Damming: NASA LHASA v2.0 steep terrain landslides impounding mountain canyons then bursting.
     5. Upstream Mega-Dam Spillway Dumps: Unannounced emergency floodgate releases from Jinghong or Xe-Pian Xe-Namnoy.
     6. Volcanic Tephra & Lahar Mudflows: Smithsonian GVP volcanic ash deposits mobilizing into hyper-dense debris slurry.
     7. Marine Tidal Lock & King Tide Backwater: South China Sea storm surge + astronomical high tide (>3.5m) halting 
        Mekong outflow, reversing river gradient, and trapping floodwaters in Phnom Penh, Kandal, and Prey Veng.
     8. Rain-on-Snow (ROS) Thermal Meltdown: Warm atmospheric rivers dumping rain onto Tibetan snowpack, transferring latent 
        heat and tripling instantaneous melt runoff into Lancang gorges.
     9. Post-Wildfire Hydrophobic Burn Scars: Waxy soil crusts creating 90%+ immediate surface runoff and reducing 
        time-of-concentration from 3 hours to 20 minutes.
     10. Urban Drainage Choke & Wetland Infill: Plastic trash clogging stormwater box culverts, pump station overload, 
         and sand-infilling of natural retention lakes (Boeng Trabek, Boeng Tamok) in Phnom Penh/Siem Reap.
     11. Watershed Deforestation & Canopy Decapitation: Loss of tree canopy and root sponge increasing the SCS Runoff 
         Curve Number (CN 55 -> 88+), causing violent mountain torrent flash surges in Pursat and Koh Kong.
     12. Linear Infrastructure Damming: Elevated national highways (NR4, NR5, NR6) acting as unengineered dams that impound 
         sheetflow until hydrostatic pressure causes sudden embankment blowout.
     13. Sand Dredging Riverbank Collapse & Avulsion: Sub-aqueous sand mining destabilizing riverbed toe (Factor of Safety < 1.0), 
         causing sudden levee collapse and diverting high-velocity Mekong flow into low-lying village depressions.
     14. Karst Conduit Subterranean Blowouts: Hydrostatic pressurization of underground limestone cave rivers during intense 
         recharge, violently erupting from sinkholes and cave springs as boiling flash floods in Kampot and Battambang."

Q11: "How are these exposed in production?"
A11: "Every driver is exposed via a dedicated REST endpoint under `/api/hazards/*`, synthesized into an aggregated
